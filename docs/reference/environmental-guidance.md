# Environmental guidance — how a level tells the player where to go

For the agent designing a campaign's places and routes: how established games guide a player through space without a marker, a label or a beam, what the principles behind those practices are, what each one is made of in Minecraft 1.21.11, what the engine lets a creator author today, and what the engine owes next. The standing rule this page serves is `game-writing.md` §8 (L1): the level carries the information and the text only restates it; guidance cues are diegetic, consistent across the design, blended into the environment and found on a close look, never a jarring highlight.

Every rule is marked **[cited]** with its source and rating, or **[authored]** — reasoned here from a cited rule or from the engine's own measured behaviour. Ratings: STRONG (a primary source — the developer's own words, a developer-authored page, or the game's own documented behaviour), MEDIUM (a secondary account of a named primary source), WEAK (one practitioner's advice or a single example, not a demonstrated practice). A source whose licence does not permit reuse is marked *ideas only* and is paraphrased, never quoted (ADR-0013, `docs/ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS.md`). The research answered the four questions it was asked and stopped; what it could not find is named in §7 so a gap is not read as absence of practice.

## 2. The yellow-paint debate, and what both sides concede

- **The objection is to the loudness, not to guidance.** [cited, STRONG] Wikipedia's record of the debate: the marker is used "because game environments often lack the realistic cues of real space"; critics say it "can break immersion, lower difficulty, and patronize skilled players"; Celia Wagar calls it a cheap fix that signals "an earlier failure in the art design of the game". The original Resident Evil 4 complaint was that the paint "seems out of place within the game's environment".
- **The defence is the observed playtest.** [cited, MEDIUM] Developers' replies were collected by Know Your Meme and others: a Ubisoft designer notes that in observed playtests players "skip past environmentally realistic props", and the alternative is a button prompt in every room; Chet Faliszek's joke that the critic "has never sat in on an observed playtest". PlaytestCloud reads the cue as born of observation, feedback and telemetry — testers "walking right past climbable objects", heat maps showing players "going back on themselves" — and names hindsight bias: "there's a good chance that many players would get stuck if it hadn't been there".
- **The maker's position is: the need is real, the argument is about execution.** [cited, STRONG] Naoki Hamaguchi (Final Fantasy VII Rebirth), after the demo: preferences differ as they do for camera options, so the team offers accessibility options and takes the feedback forward; a year later: "there is definitely a need there", and the real dispute is the method, how strong, and whether it works.
- **The resolutions on record are three.** [cited, STRONG] (1) A strength setting: Shadow of the Tomb Raider's obvious / discreet / none. (2) A diegetic replacement: Requiem's shipping seals and cable ties, "something that makes sense in the world", and light as the landing pad. (3) Fixing the art so the cue is unnecessary: Wagar's position, and Portal's advice to stand the button beside the door. Fan mods that strip the cue ("No More Yellow Paint") are the fourth, and a sign the strength was not the player's to set.
- **[authored] What this project takes from it.** A delve is played once, by a fixed party of one to four, with no settings screen the creator controls at play time: the strength is chosen at authoring and must be right for a first-time player, so the design-time answer is the discreet tier — present on the whole critical path, built from the world's own materials, and nowhere else. The dispute is settled here as L1 settles it: no marker entity, no label, no beam; a cue that a close look finds.

## 3. The principles

### 3.1 The cue families

- **Light.** [cited, MEDIUM] Players walk toward light and read lit places as safe (Gallant; the Left 4 Dead post; Rogers's post-fireworks exit). One light reads as direction; many read as decoration (Left 4 Dead). A cool accent in a warm room singles out the door (the TWHL lighting tutorial: "by using a cool light in this warm arena, we can subtly highlight the red door"; darkness is used "to de-emphasise areas"). Light also carries safety: a silhouette the player cannot see is an ambush they cannot read (Lundeen). **[authored]** Light is the first cue family because it works at every distance and in every material, and because the engine already measures it; it is placed while the room is designed, never computed afterwards (`interior-lighting.md`).
- **Colour.** [cited, STRONG] One saturated colour on the thing to use, against a palette drained of it: Mirror's Edge's red against white, Uncharted's yellow, Tomb Raider's white. The colour works by what surrounds it, not by itself (Channon: "a lot about contrast"). A colour with a prior meaning carries it in — red stopped Silli at a door. **[authored]** Colour is the loudest family and the one the debate is about; it is reserved for the smallest surface that still reads.
- **Material.** [cited, WEAK] A material that is used for one purpose and nothing else becomes a word: Requiem's cable ties and shipping seals, Tomb Raider's painted ledge, Dying Light's "visually justified parkour helper". Mojang's own trial chambers are built from one vocabulary — copper and tuff bricks, lit by copper bulbs and candles — so a player who has seen one room reads the next. **[authored]** Material is colour at low volume: a stair nosing in a block the rest of the room never uses is found on a close look and invisible to a glance, which is the register L1 asks for.
- **Shape and affordance.** [cited, STRONG] "Anything that looked climbable will be climbable" (Binkowski) is an affordance rule: the shape promises the action, and the game keeps the promise. Its inverse is Portal's: put the button beside the door and the connection needs no explanation. Climbing needs "a specific rhythm" of ledges (Zielińska) — a handhold every so often, not one. **[authored]** In a block world the affordance is the block itself: a stair is climbable, a two-block riser is not, a lever is pulled, a chest is opened. A thing that can be used must look like a thing that can be used, and a thing that looks usable must be usable (`DW0420`'s soft-lock is the invisible half of this rule; L1's "no object exists only so that reading it opens something" is the other half).
- **Composition and sightline.** [cited, MEDIUM] A triangle hides, then reveals, and draws the eye to its tip (Nintendo); a stepping stone in the middle of the path pulls the player across it (Pangilinan via GMTK); a vista framed by the opening the player walks through is the composition doing the pointing (Rogers's castle down Main Street). The Level Design Book (*ideas only*) groups composition and sightlines with lighting and signage as the coarse-grained aids most players notice, and notes that players look where they are moving and rarely up unless something draws them. **[authored]** A sightline is a promise that a distance can be closed; one that cannot (Space Mountain's spires seen from a square they cannot be reached from, in the Disneyland notes) is a lie the player pays for in minutes.
- **Motion.** [cited, MEDIUM] Birds that startle toward the goal; sparks, a flashing light, a banging door "are hard to ignore" (GMTK); a crow shot by the autogun teaches the danger (Gallant). Readers of Gallant's piece note that motion placed on the right path confirms rather than redirects. **[authored]** Motion is scarce in a block world — smoke, flowing water, fire, particles, a body moving — so one plume on a skyline is as loud as a landmark.
- **Sound.** [cited, MEDIUM] A television heard through a wall draws the player into the room (The Last of Us via GMTK); ambient sound is listed among the subtle aids in the Level Design Book (*ideas only*); this project's own record of Souls ambush cues is `souls-design-language.md` §4.3. **[authored]** Sound is directional only when the player can tell where it came from, so it is tied to a place (a bell, a fountain, a fight) and never played at the player.
- **Landmarks.** [cited, STRONG] The weenie: a tall, distinctive, visible-from-afar thing the player walks toward (Disney via Rogers and Chen; Journey's mountain; Hyrule's lit shrines and smoking stables). Lynch's *The Image of the City* names landmarks beside paths, edges, districts and nodes as the five things a mental map is made of; a district — a place with its own visual identity — tells the player where they are without a landmark. **[authored]** A box garden has one skyline; the destination the journal names (the mill, the tavern) is the landmark, built to read as what it is at playable scale (L1), and its silhouette is visible before its door is.
- **Breadcrumbs.** [cited, MEDIUM] A trail of small things toward the goal — supplies through a tunnel (Gallant), collectibles in a line, Elden Ring's golden trail. The Level Design Book (*ideas only*) rates visible rewards in the distance among the situational aids most players follow. **[authored]** A breadcrumb that is also loot moves the player and pays them; a breadcrumb that is only a mark is a sign, which L1 refuses.
- **Denial and negative space.** [cited, MEDIUM] Knowing where you cannot go says where you can: a wide pit, a barred gate, a wall; and the eye reads an opening in a dark wall as an invitation (GMTK's "negative space forming an inviting opening"; the Level Design Book's negative-reinforcement aids, *ideas only*; Totten's "denial" of a view to make its reveal land, *ideas only*). **[authored]** A barred way the player will later open (a Souls shortcut) is both a denial now and a landmark later.

### 3.2 One visual language per campaign

- **One accent, one meaning, the whole game.** [cited, STRONG] Mirror's Edge has one colour; Tomb Raider has one; the trial chambers have one palette; Portal has one pair of light colours. Zielińska's "clear visual representation" and the adventure-map advice to "pick one visual style and keep it throughout the map" say the same from two ends. **[authored]** A second accent is a second language, and the player has no dictionary: a cue family, once bound to a meaning (this block means *climb here*; this light means *the way on*), is bound for every scene of the campaign. A campaign's guidance language is declared once, at the campaign, and every place inherits it.
- **The accent lives in the world's own materials.** [cited, WEAK] Requiem's cable ties, Dying Light's justified helpers, trial-chamber copper. **[authored]** The accent block is chosen from the campaign's palette family (a stone's stair, a wood's trapdoor, a copper stage, a candle), never from the glowing or the entity class. Paving a floor with glowing ore is the type specimen of invention this project refuses (`CLAUDE.local.md`, Rulings).
- **The accent means one thing, so it appears where that thing is and nowhere else.** [authored, from Silli's red door] A cue on a surface that does not deliver — a painted ledge that cannot be climbed, a lit alcove with nothing in it — teaches the player the language lies, and every later cue is discounted. Off-route accent is a defect of the same class as an unreachable sightline.

### 3.3 Tuning discoverability against obtrusiveness

- **The strength is found by watching, not by taste.** [cited, STRONG] Every developer defence of the loud cue is an observed playtest (§2); Nintendo moved from towers to scattered attractions on a heat map; Journey was tuned on 200 testers finding the mountain. **[authored]** For this project the observer is the bot and the owner's hour: the machine proves the route is completable, and the hour is spent on whether it was found — a cue the bot does not need and the owner did not notice is tuned right.
- **Start subtle and add; never start loud and hope to remove.** [cited, MEDIUM] The Level Design Book (*ideas only*) orders aids by how many players notice them and advises adding stronger aids only where testing shows the subtle one failed; Kotaku's reading of the debate is that a toggle only works if the level was designed to work without the paint. **[authored]** A loud cue added late papers over a composition failure; the repair is to re-arrange the room, as `interior-lighting.md` rules for darkness.
- **The squint test.** [cited, MEDIUM] Blur the screen; the way on should be the brightest thing (Rogers). **[authored]** The engine's equivalent is a light reading at the decision cell toward each exit; the exit the route takes is lit at least as well as any other.
- **One cue family carries at a time.** [cited, MEDIUM] Left 4 Dead's one glow against many headlights; Mirror's Edge's one colour against none. **[authored]** Where the composition already points (one door, one light), the accent is not also laid; the accent is for the choice the composition cannot make — which of two stairs, which ledge in a wall of ledges.
- **A strength tier is a design decision, not a knob.** [cited, STRONG] Tomb Raider's three tiers exist because the studio chose each tier's look. **[authored]** This project ships one tier — discreet, on the whole critical path — and does not expose a knob; a quieter campaign hides the engine's markers (spec-0093) and relies on the level, it does not thin the accent.

### 3.4 Teaching the language early

- **The first instance is safe, unmissable and plainly rewarded.** [cited, MEDIUM] Nintendo's four-step stage (introduce, develop, twist, resolve — Hayashida's kishōtenketsu as read by Game Maker's Toolkit) opens with the mechanic where failure costs nothing; Journey's first level "is trying to teach the player about how to navigate" and later levels stop teaching; Portal's early indicator lights run from a button to a door in the same room. **[authored]** The campaign's accent appears in the first place the party stands, on a thing they must use to leave it, with nothing else competing; the second instance follows within the same scene; from the third on the player is assumed to read it.
- **Repetition is the teacher; variation is the test.** [cited, MEDIUM] "A specific rhythm" of ledges (Zielińska); the stage that reuses its idea in a harder setting (Hayashida). **[authored]** The accent is applied in the same relation to its object every time (the same course of a stair, the same side of a lever) so the player learns the relation and not the instance.
- **A landmark is shown before it is needed.** [cited, STRONG] The castle is visible down Main Street before the hub offers a choice (Rogers); the mountain is visible from the first dune (Chen); a tower "is slowly revealed" through hills and a bridge before it is reached (Nintendo). **[authored]** The destination the journal will name is in a sightline from an earlier scene, so when the NPC says "go to the mill" the player has already seen a mill.

**What a creator can do today, without engine work.** [authored] Choose one accent block family from the campaign palette and use it on the first course of every stair the route climbs and around every lever the route pulls, in every generated piece; place the accent's light (a candle, a copper bulb at the stage matching the palette) on the exit the route takes at each decision cell; give every destination a silhouette and declare a `vision` edge from the scene before it so `DW0821` proves the sightline; set `guidance.markers: hidden` once spec-0093 lands and put a `prop` on every interact; author the `hint` as a landmark the player has already seen (`quest-capabilities.md`, *Hint wording*). None of this is checked by the engine beyond the sightline and the brightness floor; that is the gap §6 closes.

## 5. Adventure-map community practice

- **The platform's own structures are the strongest exemplar in reach.** [cited, STRONG for the facts] The trial chambers (1.21) are one palette — waxed copper and tuff bricks, with copper grates and chains — lit by copper bulbs and candles, with vaults at the entrance rooms and the ominous vault over the atrium's grand staircase: one material language, light from the structure's own fittings, the reward at a landmark. **[authored]** A delve built the same way needs no yellow.
- **Mapmaker advice, where it is written down, says route, consistency and testing.** [cited, WEAK] An adventure-map guide frames the route as "breadcrumbing" — visual guides and signs that lead from start to finish; another says to pick one visual style and keep it, that the intended route should be "obvious in hindsight", and to ask testers what they think their goal is before explaining it. Neither says how to light a path. **[authored]** The community's loud cues — a glowstone floor, a beacon beam, a glowing entity over the chest — are the Minecraft yellow paint, and this project has already refused the class (L1; the glowing-palette refusal in `new-pieces.md`).
- **What was not found.** No published mapmaker's account of a *consistent accent material* across a map was found; the practice exists in the owner's observation and in Mojang's structures, not in a text this research could cite. This is recorded in §7.

## 6. What the engine does next

### 6.1 The recommendation

**[authored]** The smallest general mechanism is one declaration and three checks, binding to objects the compiler already knows.

1. **A campaign declares one guidance accent, once.** The home is the `guidance` block spec-0093 introduces at stage 5 (`markers`, `announcements`), which grows one field: `accent`, naming a palette role (spec-0050's role → block vocabulary). The role is the accent; the block it resolves to is the campaign's. No colour literal, no entity, no light value: a role, because the material family is the creator's style decision (rank-only authority, spec-0028) and the engine's business is only that the role is used where guidance is owed and nowhere else. A campaign that declares no accent is judged on nothing new.
2. **The accent's object class is the route's decision sites**, derived from the critical-path proof, not declared: every vertical leg the route climbs (a stair run or one-block step on `critical_route_cells`), every affordance the route uses (an `interact` anchor or `prop`, a `collect` container, a shortcut unlock, a trap disarm, a bonfire), and every seam the route takes out of a place with more than one exit. The compiler enumerates them and prints the count with its denominator (sites found / route legs examined). A capability belongs to the object class it acts on: the class here is "a site where the proven route asks the player to act or choose", and a hook keyed to one verb (`interact` only) is the shape to refuse.
3. **Check one — coverage.** Every decision site has at least one cell of the accent role within a stated radius (two cells, the reach of a close look at a stair's foot or a lever's surround) and that cell is lit at or above the brightness floor under the darkest sky at the step the route reaches it (the light model `DW0210` already runs), and is in the line of sight of the approach cell (`nav::walk_cells`, as `DW0821`). Refusal names the site, the step, and the nearest accent cell with its distance.
4. **Check two — purity.** No accent cell stands farther than the radius from every decision site of every proven route (mainline and optional, since optional quests have routes too). Refusal names the cell and the nearest site. This is the check only this gate can make: perturb by laying one accent block in a dead end and it must red.
5. **Check three — one language.** The role resolves to one block family across every placed piece; a piece whose palette binds the role to a block of another family is refused, naming both.
6. **Taught at the entry.** The first decision site on the mainline carries the accent and lies within the first place the party stands; refusal says the language is taught late.
7. **The remaining glowing hardware joins spec-0093.** The shortcut, bonfire and trap-disarm `item_display` icons take the same `marker` property objectives take, and an authored `prop` stands in for them, so a quiet campaign has no glowing entity at all. This is a scope extension of an approved spec, not a new mechanism.

**Why not have the compiler paint the accent.** Placement is design: which course of the stair, which side of the lever, whether the accent is the tread or the wall beside it, is what makes the cue read as part of the building rather than stuck on. The engine's standing rule for light (`interior-lighting.md`) is the precedent: the designer places, the engine checks, and a failure is repaired by re-arranging. A compiler that paints would also have to choose what to paint on, and that choice is the one §2's debate is about.

**Why a palette role and not a block.** The role is already how a piece says "this cell is the accent" without knowing the campaign (spec-0050); binding it at the campaign keeps the piece reusable across campaigns with different palettes, and makes check three a one-line resolution rather than a texture comparison.

### 6.2 What stays the creator's, and what a check can verify

| The creator decides | A check verifies |
|---|---|
| Which material family is the accent, and how loud it is against the palette | That one role is declared, resolves to one family, and is present at every decision site |
| Where within the two-cell neighbourhood the accent sits, and on which face | That the cell is lit and in sight from the approach |
| Which places are landmarks and what they look like | That a declared vision edge is unobstructed (`DW0821`) and that the destination of the next objective is in a declared sightline from the completing scene — a derived `vision` obligation the layout gate can state |
| Whether a scene is deliberately unguided (a maze) | Nothing — there is no opt-out in the first cut; a maze is authored as one decision site at its entry and one at its exit, which the route proof already separates, and the question is re-opened on a demo level if a campaign needs it |
| The sound a place makes and the smoke it sends up | That a guidance sound is played at an anchor, not at the player |

### 6.3 Acceptance criteria, sketched

Numbers (DW codes, `dsl_version`) are allocated by the planner; none are named here.

1. `delvec schema --stage quests` exports `guidance.accent`; the gallery binds it and perturbing the role's block moves an emitted byte; a document without it canonicalises byte-identically.
2. A fixture whose route climbs two stairs and pulls one lever, all accented, builds green and prints the binding line (sites / legs).
3. Removing the accent from one stair's foot reds, naming the site and step; moving the accent to a cell the approach cannot see reds; darkening the accent cell below the floor reds.
4. Laying one accent block in a dead end reds, naming the cell and the nearest site.
5. Binding the role to two block families across two pieces reds, naming both.
6. A fixture whose first accent appears in the second place reds as taught late.
7. A `shortcut` with `marker: hidden` and a `prop` emits no `item_display`; the gallery probe for the unlock still walks.
8. A demo level row *Environmental guidance* in `docs/demo-levels.md`: a three-scene delve with one accent family, no marker entity, a landmark seen from scene one and reached in scene three; the bot walks it, and the owner's hour is spent on whether the cue was found.
9. `quest-capabilities.md` teaches the accent beside the `guidance` block, and this page's §4 table gains a column entry for it.

## 7. What could not be researched

- No developer statement on Dying Light's paint colour on climbable edges was found; the owner's example stands as an observation. The rules quoted from Techland are about affordance and rhythm, not colour.
- The GDC 2018 level-design workshop *Invisible Intuition* (Shaver, Yang) is the closest primary source on guidance without HUD aids; its slides are behind the GDC Vault's membership wall and only the session description was read.
- Nintendo's CEDEC 2017 talk was read through two secondary accounts (a live-tweet record and a Game Maker's Toolkit article), not the slides.
- No written mapmaker practice of a consistent accent material across a Minecraft adventure map was found; the community advice found is light-and-landmark, one blog each, rated WEAK.
- The Level Design Book is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 and is used as ideas only, paraphrased; its wayfinding page is the best single survey found and a reader who can accept the licence should read it directly.
