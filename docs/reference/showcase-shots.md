# Showcase shots

How a storybook or front-page picture of a built delve is composed: where the camera stands, what it looks at, how wide it sees, and how the picture is judged. Agent-facing. The tool is `delvec cameras` ([`tools.md`](tools.md) §4a); this page is the craft it serves.

Every rule is marked **[cited]** with its source, or **[authored]** — reasoned here from a cited rule or from what the engine's renders measurably do. A source read only through a search-result summary is marked *(summary only)*.

## 1. What "at its best" means

A showcase picture shows **one built thing, close, from the side that says what it is, at render quality**. It is judged by looking at it, never by a number: a frame can hold the whole subject box inside a third of its area and still be a picture of grounds. **[authored]**

- **The approved concept image teaches the kind of view, not the pose.** What was built differs from what was drawn; a camera that chases the drawing's exact angle reproduces a picture of a different building. Take from it: oblique or square-on, how close, how high, what the room's point of interest is. **[authored]**
- **A showcase camera is placed where the picture is best, not where a body can stand.** In the air, above a courtyard, in a corner above head height, outside a window. The walkable model, the anchor cell and the blind-frame report (`DW0893`) are review instruments for a player's view; none of them constrains a showcase camera. **[authored]**

## 2. Exteriors

1. **Fill the frame with the building; the surroundings are margin.** Filling the frame with the subject is the architectural photographer's choice for impact; framing it within its surroundings is the other choice, made for context, and a front-page hero is not a context shot. **[cited]** Contrastly, [*6 Can't-Miss Tips for Better Composition of Architecture Photos*](https://contrastly.com/6-cant-miss-tips-for-better-composition-of-architecture-photos/) — "filling the frame with the building", and "occasionally framing buildings … within their surroundings". No source gives a fill percentage; that is why none is a verdict here. **[authored]**
2. **Name the building, not the site.** A site's box includes its grounds, ditches and terraces, so a frame fitted to it is a frame of grounds with a building in the middle. `delvec panorama --subject anchor/<a>,…` fits to the span of the anchors that stand inside the building; a stated camera needs no subject at all. **[authored]** — judged on doune: fitted to the layout box, the north-east frames were mostly valley.
3. **Shoot the three-quarter view: two faces at once.** A corner view shows depth and massing; a square-on elevation is for symmetry or one face's detail. **[cited]** *(summary only)* architectural-visualization guidance collected by search: "a three-quarter … view is a 45° hero shot showing two sides at once"; "in a frontal, or elevation, view the camera sits perpendicular to the wall". The yaw is continuous: a view is rarely square to a compass direction. **[authored]**
4. **Stand further back with a narrower field of view rather than close with a wide one.** A wide lens close to a building exaggerates perspective; the photographer controls perspective by standpoint and focal length together. **[cited]** Adrian Schulz, [*Architectural Photography* (3rd ed.)](https://www.amazon.com/Architectural-Photography-3rd-Composition-Processing/dp/1937538761), chapters "The Effects of Focal Length" and "Camera Standpoint" (table of contents read; text not read). For a 16:9 Chunky frame a vertical `fov` of 40–56° from 60–110 blocks shows a castle-sized building without visible keystoning. **[authored]** — measured on doune's hero frames.
5. **Light the side you show.** The declared hour fixes the sun; a bearing that looks into it is a backlit frame, and a face in its own shadow reads as a flat grey slab. **[authored]** Directional light that stacks light and dark guides the eye. **[cited]** *(summary only)* environment-art lighting interviews (exp-points.com, "Asking the Legends – Lighting Art").
6. **Lift the camera a little above the building's base; keep pitch small.** A level camera keeps verticals vertical; a small downward pitch shows the roofs and the courtyard behind the front without the plan-like look of a steep one. **[cited]** HDRsoft, [*What's the Best Height for Shooting Real Estate Interiors?*](https://www.hdrsoft.com/learn/best-height-for-shooting-real-estate-interiors.html) — "if they are leaning inwards or outwards noticeably … your camera is probably at the wrong elevation". Steeper than about 30° a frame reads as a model on a table; the "from above" shot is a separate, second picture. **[authored]**

## 2a. The whole-map hero

A front page for a map that is the point shows a different subject from §2: **the whole place at once, far enough back to show its scale, near enough that its parts still read as buildings.** §2.1–2.2 fill the frame with one building; this picture fills it with the site. Two limits bind it: not so far that the castle becomes a small shape with no legible detail, and not so close that only a fragment shows. **[authored]**

1. **Show every part, and how the parts stand to each other.** An establishing shot "sets up, or establishes, the context for a scene by showing the relationship between its important figures and objects", as "a long or extreme-long shot". **[cited]** Wikipedia, [*Establishing shot*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Establishing_shot). For a delve that means the first place a player reaches and the last building they climb in one frame — on vesperhold the wayside shrine, the causeway, the gatehouse, the ward, the keep and the bell tower. **[authored]**
2. **Let the route lead to the castle.** Showing a castle in the distance with a path leading up to it is how game artists give a large castle its scale — "a sort of playable establishing shot". **[cited]** Adventure Gamers, [*A Look at Graphics: Details in the Distance*](https://adventuregamers.com/article/a-look-at-graphics-details-in-the-distance). Leading lines guide the eye to the subject, and a subject on a vanishing point draws it hardest. **[cited]** Neil Oseman, [*Composing a Wide Shot*](https://neiloseman.com/composing-a-wide-shot/). The route the party walks is the line: its start in a lower corner, the castle at its far end. **[authored]**
3. **Pin a corner; close one side.** "Find an element that I can place just within a corner of the frame to anchor the composition", best when "the diagonally opposite corner is opposite in other ways too – dark vs. light, close vs. far"; a vertical element closing one side makes an L-shaped frame. **[cited]** Oseman, same article. On vesperhold the lit shrine pins the lower right corner against the dark keep at the upper left, and the valley wall closes the right side. **[authored]**
4. **Scale comes from air and from repetition into the distance.** "Atmospheric Fog is extremely important for creating distance. It's good to balance the fog, sky, and terrain to have different values", and the same element seen near and far reads as far away. **[cited]** Tony Arechiga in 80.lv, [*Creating Breathtaking Game Backgrounds*](https://80.lv/articles/creating-breathtaking-game-backgrounds). Distant things are paler and lower in contrast than near ones. **[cited]** *(summary only)* Wikipedia, *Aerial perspective*. The fog that does this is §2b's; the hero adds none of its own. **[authored]**
5. **Stand where the frame is about as wide as the site, measured at the site's centre.** Project the site's box onto the frame's horizontal axis; the frame's width at the depth of the site's centre, 2 · distance · tan(hfov / 2), should be 1.0–1.1 times that extent, with the camera 60–100 blocks above the level the castle stands on and pitch 18–23°. **[authored]** — measured on vesperhold (site 172 × 292 blocks): at 1.06 (215 blocks back, `fov` 48) every part reads and the shrine sits inside its corner; at 0.92 (199 back, `fov` 45) the shrine and the causeway's foot fall out of frame; at 1.18–1.2 (289–293 back, `fov` 40) the whole is there but the castle is a grey band across the upper half with no legible roofline.
6. **Keep what makes it recognisable in the middle.** Store key art is "a visually rich image that is easily recognizable", with critical content inside a central safe area because the frame is cropped as it scales. **[cited]** Steamworks, [*Library Assets*](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/assets/libraryassets). The castle and the route sit inside the middle of the frame; the edges are valley and sky. **[authored]**
7. **Choose the bearing whose side carries the landmarks and the light.** On vesperhold the chapel's rose window, the cloister and the bell tower stand on the west half, and the dusk sun is in the west (§2.5): from the south-west they face the lens and the causeway runs as one diagonal from the shrine in the lower right corner to the gate; from the south and south-east the keep's roof stands in front of them and the lit faces turn away; from due west the route is edge-on. **[authored]** — judged on six bearings: south-east, south-south-east, due south, south-south-west, south-west and west.
8. **One frame, not a composite.** Composites of several exposures exist in landscape photography as *perspective blends*: a distant subject that a wide lens shrinks is replaced by a longer-lens frame of the same view, and the failure is the seam between the two focal planes. **[cited]** Fstoppers, [*How to Blend Multiple Perspectives for Dramatic Photos*](https://fstoppers.com/landscapes/how-blend-multiple-perspectives-dramatic-photos-572570). A montage of several angles is another kind of picture: it lays parts side by side and cannot show how they stand to each other, which is what rule 1 asks. **[authored]** — tried on vesperhold: the hero at three quarters of the width beside a column of three crops (the bell tower, the approach, the ward) shrank the whole to make room for parts the whole already showed, and each crop, a quarter of the width, is about 220 pixels wide where the front page is displayed; the single frame was kept.

## 2b. The sky a picture is taken under

The design states a time and a weather per picture; the renderer carries the time and not the weather. `delvec cameras` sets Chunky's sun from the declared hour (dusk: altitude 0.2169 rad, about 12.4°, azimuth π, the west) and leaves the sky at Chunky's default, a clear simulated sky; rain and thunder have no Chunky counterpart and are not drawn. **[authored]** — read off the emitted scene.

1. **Overcast dusk, as vesperhold's pictures are taken:** `sky.mode` `SOLID_COLOR`, `sky.color` (0.10, 0.11, 0.14), `sky.skyLight` 1.6, `sky.apparentSkyLight` 0.6; `sun.intensity` 0.25, `sun.color` (0.9, 0.75, 0.65), `sun.drawTexture` false, the sun's position left where the hour put it; `fog.mode` `UNIFORM`, `fog.uniformDensity` 0.002, `fog.color` (0.20, 0.22, 0.26), `fog.skyFogDensity` 0. These are Chunky's scene keys, set on the emitted scene beside the camera `delvec cameras` wrote; no block light is added or removed (§3.7). **[authored]** — chosen on vesperhold's aerial and hero frames, by look, over four rounds:
   - Chunky's default sky reads as a clear summer evening: a warm band on the horizon and hard shadows.
   - A grey `SOLID_COLOR` sky (0.30, 0.33, 0.38) with the sun at 0.3 reads overcast but pale: the sky is a blank white-grey third of the frame.
   - While `skyFogDensity` is 1 the fog's colour paints the visible sky, so darkening `sky.color` alone did not darken it; with `skyFogDensity` 0 it did.
   - `apparentSkyLight` 0.6 darkens the sky the lens sees to slate without darkening what the sky lights; at 1.0 it stayed pale.
   - A `GRADIENT` sky (dark top, lighter horizon) looked the same as the solid one under this fog, and was dropped.
   - Uniform fog 0.006 turned the castle 250 blocks away into a grey silhouette; 0.002 separates the valley wall from the castle without hiding either.
   - A `LAYERED` fog band at the valley floor (y 72, breadth 10, density 0.04) drew no visible mist, so the concept images' valley mist is not in the pictures.
2. **Expose a dusk exterior at 2.0 under that sky.** At 1.0 the castle's walls sink into one dark tone; the drafts at 1.5–1.8 read, and the final frames take 2.0. **[authored]** — measured on vesperhold.
3. **1024 spp for an overcast dusk exterior.** Seven 1600 × 900 frames took 13 min 52 s to 17 min 57 s each at 9 threads and show no grain at full size; a lower count was not tried. **[authored]** — measured on vesperhold.
4. **One sky for the whole set.** Every picture of one delve takes the same block, so the set reads as one place at one hour; in a candle-lit room it changes nothing visible, and a window or a broken roof shows the same sky. **[authored]**
5. **A frame may take a different sky when the declared one leaves the room unreadable, and its caption says so.** **[authored]** — vesperhold's chapel is shown at a clear noon (Chunky's default simulated sky, sun altitude π/2, fog 0), exposure 10, 1024 spp plus BM3D: against a 4096-spp reference of the same 400 × 225 frame the rms error was 7.9 at 300 spp, 5.3 at 300 plus BM3D, 4.7 at 1024 plus BM3D and 4.5 at 4096 plus BM3D.

## 3. Interiors

1. **Shoot from a corner, into the room: two-point perspective.** A camera square to a wall gives one vanishing point and a flat, formal frame; aimed into a corner, each wall has its own vanishing point and the room reads in depth. **[cited]** Architizer, [*7 Rules for Composing Powerful Architectural Perspectives*](https://architizer.com/blog/practice/tools/the-art-of-rendering-perspectives/) — "I typically set my 3D model software (SketchUp) to 'Two Point Perspective'". Do not back so far into the corner that the three far corners crowd the frame. **[cited]** *(summary only)* interior real-estate composition guidance: "shooting from one corner so you can fit the other three corners into the shot just looks wrong".
2. **Put the room's point of interest where the lines lead.** Fireplace, high table, altar, hearth: the strongest element dominates the frame hierarchy and the perspective lines converge on it. **[cited]** Architizer, same article — "the focal point of the image … is hierarchically stronger than all other elements", "the perspective lines converge on the bottom right third". Tables, benches and ceiling beams are the leading lines a Minecraft room has. **[authored]**
3. **Layer foreground, middle ground and background.** A near object framing the view, the room's use in the middle, the point of interest behind. **[cited]** 80.lv, [*Get Away: Lighting & Composition in Environment Art*](https://80.lv/articles/get-away-lighting-composition-in-environment-art) — "elements in the foreground to frame the main visual interest and add more depth to the scene".
4. **Camera height: chest height by default, higher when the floor is the room.** Interiors are photographed from about 1.2–1.5 m, a standing eye's height. **[cited]** PhotographyForRealEstate.net, [*How High Should Your Camera Be When Shooting Real Estate Interiors?*](https://photographyforrealestate.net/how-high-should-your-camera-be-when-shooting-real-estate-interiors/) — "set the shooting height around 4 to 5 feet". Getting up high or down low makes a more interesting composition. **[cited]** 80.lv, same article — "By getting up high or down low, you are able to create a more interesting composition." A hall whose tables, hearth and dais lie on the floor is shown from 5–8 blocks up with a 12–18° pitch. **[authored]**
5. **Use a moderately wide lens and no wider.** Interiors are photographed at 16–35 mm full-frame; the widest end distorts the frame's edges. **[cited]** Fotober, [*Choosing the Best Focal Length for Real Estate Photography*](https://fotober.com/best-focal-length-for-real-estate-photography) — "Wide-angle lenses, typically ranging from 16mm to 35mm", "the very low end of this range (like 16mm) requires caution". A 16:9 frame at vertical `fov` 60–74° covers that range. **[authored]** A game's own play FOV is chosen for awareness, and a still reads warped at it. **[cited]** *(summary only)* Polycount, [*Environment Artist Portfolio Tips*](https://polycount.com/discussion/220262/environment-artist-portfolio-tips).
6. **Crop for scale: leave floor and ceiling, cut nothing at a corner.** A shape that fuses with the frame edge or sits in a corner reads as an accident. **[cited]** Zoner Learn, [*Cropping Photos: Guidelines for Professional Results*](https://learn.zoner.com/cropping-photos-guidelines-for-professional-results/) — shapes that "fuse with the edge of your frame". An empty floor or ceiling taking a third of the frame is dead space; tilt or rise until the room's furnishing fills it. **[authored]**
7. **Expose for the room.** A torch- and slit-window-lit Minecraft room reaches a path tracer's lens far dimmer than the game draws the same light levels. The camera's `exposure` (8–16 for doune's rooms) is stated per camera and puts no light into the world; the room's lighting is never changed for a picture. **[authored]** — measured: doune's great hall at exposure 1 and 1024 spp reads only near its torches.
8. **Sample budget, showcase only: 300 spp in daylight; 4096 spp plus a BM3D pass in a torch-lit room.** A 400×225 crop of doune's great hall against an 8192-spp reference (its own noise, about 4.6 in 8-bit rms, removed): raw error 9.0 at 2048 spp and 6.5 at 4096; after `ffmpeg … bm3d=sigma=45` on 2048 spp 5.7, after `bm3d=sigma=30` on 4096 spp 4.4. By eye on the full frame, 1240 spp plus BM3D smears floor tiles into blotches, 2048 is slightly blotchy and soft, 4096 is clean. Chunky's emitter sampling `ONE`/`ONE_BLOCK` and sun sampling `IMPORTANCE` bought no noise per second of render. On a ten-core machine 1000 spp at 1600×900 is about 21 minutes, so one interior is about 90 minutes: this budget is for the storybook's few pictures, never for a creator's working loop, where `--preview` (seconds) and `--draft` (128 spp, about 20 s) place and light a camera. **[authored]** — measured on doune on the pinned core.

9. **A candle-lit room: exposure 16–24, and the full 4096 spp.** Candles are small emitters that a path finds only by chance, so at 128 spp a candle-lit hall is sparks on black, and at 512 it still is. The walls are not dim but unsampled: doubling the exposure from 12 to 24 moved the frame's mean luma only from 28 to 33 (8-bit). At 4096 spp and exposure 16 the same hall reads — walls, floor and the broken roof — at mean luma 37.5. The sparks are the same with fog off. **[authored]** — measured on vesperhold's great hall, 400 × 225, pinned core; the emitter-sampling measurement is in [`tools.md`](tools.md) §4a. A 1600 × 900 candle-lit frame at 4096 spp took 2 h 13 min at 9 threads. After `bm3d=sigma=30` the frame looks flatter, because the speckle that read as contrast is gone; its mean luma is unchanged (43.22 raw, 43.19 denoised on vesperhold's pool), and running the filter on RGB planes (`format=gbrp,bm3d=sigma=30,format=rgb24`) gives the same luma, so the flatness is not a colour conversion.

## 4. How a camera is found

**Every approved image is answered, and the engine holds the record to it.** One camera per row of `design.json` — a row may have several, a camera answers exactly one — or `delvec build` refuses the campaign (`DW0900`) and `delvec cameras` writes no scene. Cameras are placed after the first build, against that build; a campaign that has placed none builds and is stopped at the staging gate. Never re-aim a camera at a second picture to clear the count: a row keeps its `answers`, and another picture is another row. **[authored]**

1. **Estimate from the approved image**: which side, how high, how close, how wide, what is the point of interest. Write it into `design/cameras.json` as `source: estimated`.
2. **Place it with `--preview`** — the assembled world flat-lit in seconds, the same camera convention as `snapshot --camera` — until the camera is in the room and the point of interest is in frame.
3. **Bracket it**: `--bracket yaw=,pitch=,fov=,dolly=,truck=,rise=` emits the camera moved one thing at a time; `delvec contact-sheet` lays the candidates on one page beside the approved image. Pick by looking; write the pick with `delvec place-camera <campaign> --name <row> --candidates candidates.json --pick <candidate>`.
4. **Draft it in Chunky** (`--draft`, quarter size, 128 spp) to judge light and exposure, then render the stated frame.
5. **Look at the final frame** against §2, §2a or §3 and write down what is in it, what is not, and why it beat the others. A frame the author would not put on a front page is not handed over. **[authored]**
6. **When no estimate is the picture, the person places the camera.** They stand in the running game where the shot should be taken and fire `/trigger dw.cam`; `delvec place-camera --report` writes exactly that pose as `source: hand`, and it is final until they delete it — no estimate replaces it. The loop is [`tools.md`](tools.md) §4a. **[authored]**

## 5. What is not researched here

- No source gives a subject-fill percentage, and none was adopted.
- No source for a vertical-field-of-view table per focal length was read; the `fov` ranges above are measured on renders, not converted from lenses.
- Game showcase practice (FOV below play FOV, lighting direction) was read only through summaries.
- No source gives a distance or frame-width ratio for a whole-map establishing shot; §2a.5's is measured on one site, vesperhold.
- No source on reproducing weather in Chunky was read; §2b's values are chosen by look on one delve.
