# A place reads as what it is

Reader: the agent writing one place's program at step 9, and the engine work that hands it a frame. This is the research record behind spec-0123: how big a building is cut, how it is divided into rooms, how it shows its trade from the threshold and from the street, what makes its outside look lived in, what a way owes its approach, and how the parts of one building are kept in one style so that a front, and a landmark, read as what they are (§7), and how a plot — a place that holds one or more related structures and the ground they share — is planned with what it holds before it is sized, at the player building community's scale (§8), and how a whole town is laid out and judged (§10). Every rule is marked **[cited]** with its source, **[authored]** — reasoned here from a cited rule or from the engine, with no source of its own — or **[ruling]** — a finding of the side-by-side playtest of one hillside town built twice, taken as given. Sources are read for their rules; nothing of their text, images or builds is taken (ADR-0013: an unlicensed or non-commercial source is ideas only). Rooflines and facades are `roof-and-facade-craft.md`; light is `interior-lighting.md`; the width of a way is `clear-width.md`.

## 2. The cut and the size

**[ruling]** A site is cut with **one box per plot or connector**; this cut's layout and seams were judged better than a cut of two terraces holding several buildings each. A road, a flight of steps, a bridge and a square's centrepiece are each a place with a box of their own. A plot is a place too: it holds one or more structures that belong together and the ground they share (§8), and a building standing alone on no ground of its own is a plot of one structure.

**[cited]** A village's buildings each connect to the street and not usually to each other; a town has larger open centres and denser structures; a settlement reads as genuine when it has a market place or, failing that, small trade shops. (Settlement guide.) A yard — a square space out back for vegetables, washing, animals or storage — is part of a house where the plot allows. (WesterosCraft.)

**[ruling]** A building's play space is **about twice** what the first cut gave it, so its author has room for rooms. The first cut's trade buildings stood on 64 to 121 cells of play space (8 × 8 to 11 × 11, read from its site plan); the rule puts a trade building at about 130 to 240 cells.

**[ruling]** The ruling above was carried out as a bigger box per building, every building a plain rectangle with no ground of its own, and that was wrong. A place's size is never room for its main building alone, and never bigger for its own sake: an oversized plot spreads its structures too far apart or makes one too large against a body. **The plot's size follows from what it holds**, planned at the cut — which structures, which open spaces, each with its use and its share — and inside the plot the structures take any outline. §8 is the research and the rule.

**[authored]** From the two rulings and §3's room rule: a structure a trade is worked in has play space for at least two rooms — about 120 to 320 cells for the work and the front together, the community's own band (§8.2) — and it is one part of its plot, never the plot's whole box; the plot's play space is the sum of its parts and the ground between them (§8.5). These are the numbers a plot's program states per part, and the engine holds the plot to them (spec-0125); no figure is a class the engine infers from a label.

## 3. Rooms, and reading as a trade

**[cited]** Divide the interior into distinct rooms with specific purposes before any wall goes up; place them as a household used them — work and storage downstairs, sleeping upstairs, the kitchen at the hearth; a single undivided room rarely reads as a lived-in space, and a two-block ceiling reads as cramped. (WesterosCraft.) Ceilings of three to four blocks feel spacious; hallways are two wide in a small house and three or four in a large one; a kitchen is a raised table, a sink, a range and a worktop, a bedroom a bed, a chest dresser and something on the wall. (Adding beauty.)

**[cited]** Most householders worked from home: a carpenter's downstairs workshop, a blacksmith's tools at the hearth, a weaver's loom. Every block serves a purpose; a small detail that shows occupancy — one mug on a barrel — beats a pile of crates that only takes up space. (WesterosCraft.)

**[cited]** In environmental storytelling the elements combine into a larger picture while each has significance on its own; the player assembles *what happened here* from the objects and their arrangement, and a scene that says nothing about the premise wastes its place. (Worch and Smith.)

**[cited]** For a public that could not read, a picture hung over the street named the trade — the bush of a tavern, the pole of a barber, the three balls of a pawnbroker; hanging signs became so common that cities limited their size and projection. (Signage.)

**[ruling]** A plain house with a loom and wool dumped in it does **not** read as a weaver's. An open-walled smithy, and a cartographer's shop with a reception room in front of an inner map room, do read as their trades. The best single room seen was a spawn-side room with a table and carpet.

**[authored]** The rule, from the four citations and the ruling. A building reads as its trade when **four things hold at once**:

1. **The working object stands in its working position**, not stored: the loom with a web on it and the bench before it, the anvil beside the forge with the quench at hand, the drawing table under the window with the finished map on the wall. A tool on a shelf is storage; a tool in use is a trade.
2. **The rooms follow the work**: the room a customer enters is not the room the work is done in, and the door between them says so — a counter room and a workroom, a shop and a store, a kitchen and a hall. A trade building has at least two rooms.
3. **The front says the trade before the door is opened**: a sign of the trade's own object hung over the street, the goods shown at a window or a counter, or the workroom itself open to the street where the trade was worked in the open (a smithy, a stall).
4. **Every room has a use a player could name**, and nothing is filler: a crate stack, a lone furnace, a bare bed in a shop are the cells the ruling judged bad.

**[authored]** The test, at the threshold: stand in the door and name the trade with no sign read. A frame from the threshold is the engine's picture of this test (spec-0123 §3.4), and the review names the trade from the frame before it reads the label.

## 4. Exterior life

**[cited]** A garden of shrubs, flowers, a few trees and potted plants; a hedge of one or two leaf blocks on a log; stairs and slabs as edging; worn floors in cracked and mossy variants where feet pass; a wall that looks empty gets something hung on it. (Adding beauty.) An animal pen, a vegetable patch or a wood-chopping place gives a plain house character. (WesterosCraft.)

**[ruling]** The first cut had too few of these: bushes, potted flowers and non-functional props that make the outside feel lived in.

**[authored]** Where the outside lives in this engine: a street's verge (the columns of a way place's extent outside its `clear`, `clear-width.md` §3), a yard or garden strip as a ground part of its plot (§8, spec-0125) or, where it belongs to no plot — a square's green — as its own open-sided place (spec-0123 §5), and the commons dressed by the stage-7 edit script (`scatter`, `plant`, `fragment`). Nothing stands in a way's clear width; a prop against a house wall stands in the street's verge, so the street's author dresses the house's foot, and the handout tells it what house it is.

## 5. The approach to a wide way

**[cited]** A stairway landing is as wide as the stair it serves and as deep as the stair's width (to 48 inches); a door onto it never takes more than half of it. (IBC §1011.6, through `clear-width.md`.) The engine confirms a way's declared width along its whole corridor and **one approach cell** at each end (spec-0120 §3).

**[ruling]** A three-wide grand stair with a one-wide crooked approach in one room is a defect; in the case seen a newel and a fireplace left one cell. A wide way's approach is as wide as the way, at least three; features such as a hearth and a stair stand three to five cells apart.

**[authored]** The engine judges the width and the first approach cell; the depth of the approach beyond that cell is the author's, from the ruling: a landing as deep as the way is wide, and nothing fixed — a hearth, a newel, a pillar — inside that landing. A newel stands beside the stair's width (`clear-width.md` §3), and a hearth's cheeks stand outside the landing.

## 6. The ground at a seam

**[cited]** A change in level above half an inch on an accessible route is a ramp, not a step (ADA 303); a change of less than twelve inches in a path of travel is a slope, and a single riser is admitted only where a code names it. (IBC §1003.5.)

**[ruling]** At a seam between places the ground must not leave a trench that a body walking the main road falls into and must detour to get out of.

**[authored]** The ring row across a seam at grade is walked at the sill: both pieces draw their ground to the sill at the crossing, and the side the plan hands the row to paves it. A cell of a way's corridor lower than the cells before and after it along the travel is a trench; `DW0921` asks a different question — whether a body is soft-locked — and a trench a body can jump out of is not a pocket, so its zero is not evidence against the ruling. What a check would measure is spec-0123 §11.

## 7. One building, one style

**[ruling]** The parts of one building, and of one frontage, are visually coherent with each other: in proportion, in height rhythm, and in the consistency of material and element. The choice of door, opening or counter follows from how that building is constructed, and a different way of building makes a different door the right one. A landmark must read as what it is. The case judged: an open smithy front whose counter stood one course high with a two-course door beside it, both one cell in from a five-course open face with air over both, so the door read as abrupt; and a square's fountain, a flat ring of stone round one cell of water at the rim's own height, which read as a well. The rule is systemic and is **not** written per building type — no "a smithy takes a fence gate".

**[cited]** Order gives due measure to the members of a work considered separately; eurythmy is beauty and fitness in the adjustments of the members, when they all correspond symmetrically; symmetry is a proper agreement between the members of the work itself, and the relation between the different parts and the whole, in accordance with a certain part selected as standard. (Vitruvius.) Beauty results from the form and correspondence of the whole with respect to the several parts, of the parts with regard to each other, and of these again to the whole; walls are built so that the solid stands upon the solid and the void over the void, the openings of doors and windows exactly over one another; a principal door is about twice as high as it is wide; the windows of one storey are all equal, though the rooms behind them differ; the storey above has windows a sixth less in the height of the void than those beneath; a jamb is no less than a sixth and no more than a fifth of the opening. (Palladio.) Relationships between a building's parts are governed by multiples of a standard unit, the module. (Proportion.) A facade is counted in bays, the space between posts or columns, and where there are only regularly spaced windows each window is one bay. (Bay.)

**[cited]** Openings define a building's character by their shape and their rhythm — a run grouped 4, 3, 4, 1, 4 over a run of 1, 1, 1 — and by the relationship between the width of the openings and the wall space between them; inserting a window into the gap, or painting the window heads to match the wall, changes the whole; a modest front may have a three-part shape whose centre bay is the controlling aspect; a hierarchy makes the front's windows more important than the side's; a rhythm is so much of a thing's character that breaking a few of its repeats alters it. (Brief 17.) A storefront is bulkheads, display windows, transoms, a signboard under a cornice, and the piers or thin iron posts that frame them; its entrance is differentiated from the others and often recessed; "most storefront frames are generally composed of horizontal and vertical elements"; a new front respects the scale and proportion of the building, keeps the horizontal separation between the storefront and the storeys above, places its doors where they relate to the windows above, avoids too many different colours on one facade and carries one colour scheme over every painted element to unify upper and lower; the question asked of any front is whether it and the floors above were created as an overall design or are very different and unrelated; a front dressed in a borrowed vocabulary — "lumberyard colonial" on a commercial building — is wrong whatever its craft. (Brief 11.)

**[cited]** The shopfront descends from the market stall set into the ground floor: a medieval shopfront was two or more unglazed openings with arched heads and shutters, with a low shelf pegged to the structure; the unglazed front stayed an integral part of the building's structural frame and its cill grew into a fully developed counter; a shutter hinged at the bottom folded down to form the counter; pilasters were introduced to carry the upper storeys; when plate glass arrived the upper storeys looked as if they sat unsupported on a void, and pilasters, consoles and cornices were added to terminate the front and give it visual support — a heavy structure on a flimsy sheet below is invariably unsuccessful. The stallriser was the display table and forms a solid base; its material matches the shopfront and the building above; the cill's size depends on the scale and proportion of the other details; the door's design reflects the other elements; the console's choice reflects the details used in the other elements; the fascia is in proportion to the shopfront and to the height of the building and sits below the first-floor cills. (Canterbury; buildinghistory.) A lintel is the beam that spans an opening, an arch is a curved lintel, and a sill lies below. (Lintel.) A Dutch door is divided so that the bottom leaf may remain shut while the top leaf opens — a door that is also a window, which kept animals out and let light and air in. (Dutch door.)

**[cited]** In the game: one colour of timber and daub per build, two complementary woods at most, no long unbroken run of one block, and depth only with a structural reason — a sill, a reveal, a buttress, the eave — because depth that only breaks a flat wall looks arbitrary (WesterosCraft; held in `roof-and-facade-craft.md` §6); the palette is carried into the roof, so a house of oak and spruce planks gets no acacia ceiling; depth is crucial; a window one or two blocks above the ground is easy to see out of; too many doors make a house look designed rather than lived in. (Adding beauty.) The game's own well, the plains meeting point, is a cobblestone ring round four cells of water two deep, the ring standing two courses above the ground, four fence posts two tall on it and a cobblestone slab for a roof; the game's own fountain is a cobblestone stack with a water source on its top at the third course, pouring down. (Village blueprints.) The community's fountain is a frame of at least 5 × 5 round a column three high with water poured on the column's top so that it runs down every side, the frame at least four wider than the column, placed at the centre of a garden, a yard or a park. (Building water features.)

**[cited]** A fountain is a decorative reservoir used for discharging water, or a structure that jets water into the air; until the modern pump every fountain ran by gravity, so its source stood higher than it, and a village fountain kept a vessel of water above its spouts so the water fell from the spouts into the basin below. (Fountain.) A well's lining is extended upward above the ground to form a wall around the hole, a roof is hung over it, and the water is raised in a bucket. (Water well.) A landmark is an external point of reference at least one aspect of which is unique or memorable in its context; the most successful node is unique in some way. (Image of the City; Imageability.) What reads is judged from the player's own position — an oblique angle makes a door nearly invisible; a screenshot taken to black and white and blurred shows where the eye lands; pretend to be a new player. (Readability.)

**[authored]** The rule, from the citations and the ruling, in four parts. A building is in one style when all four hold:

1. **One module.** Every part of a building is measured in the unit the body gives it: a **course** (a counter, a sill, a step, a stallriser), a **body** of two courses (a door, a window's clear height, the gap under a beam a body walks through), a **storey** (the clearance). Parts that share a wall share the unit and meet at its lines: the counter's top is the door's hand-height, the door's head is the window's head, the storey line is the eave or the beam. A part that stands in no line — a one-course counter and a two-course door with nothing over either under a five-course void — is the miss the ruling names.
2. **Openings follow construction.** An opening is a hole in a wall, so it has jambs and a head; what fills it follows how the wall is built, and the leaf is chosen last. A walled front takes a door in the wall and a window beside it. A front that is a frame of posts and a beam takes a counter under the beam and no door — the body walks round the counter's end, as at a stall. A timber-framed front takes a wide shuttered opening whose sill has grown into the counter, and a half-door beside it under the same head. A masonry front takes an arch with the counter under it. A front with no wall at all — an open side — must build the frame that the wall would have been, one cell inside the open ring, and stand its counter and its door in that frame: a door standing in air is a door with no wall, and the build's census writes it so for the review to read (spec-0123 §3.5). No door is named per trade; the construction names it.
3. **Lines run through.** The heads of a front's openings stand at one course, or in a hierarchy the author means (the principal door taller than the rest, Palladio's rule), and a horizontal — the beam, the fascia, the eave — terminates the front over every opening; posts stand under posts, solid upon solid and void over void. The trim that frames one opening frames every opening on that front; one structural material and one infill per building, and a facade of more than three block kinds is read for whether each kind has a job. A front and the storeys above it are one design, never two.
4. **A landmark reads by its silhouette from the approach.** A body reads shape before detail, at eye height, from the cell where it enters the place; what the thing does must be visible in the silhouette. Water that stands above the ground and falls says fountain; water below a rim under a roof on posts says well; a figure on a plinth above eye height says statue. The parts of a landmark obey parts 1–3; a flat ring round a cell of water at the rim's own height is neither shape and reads as the nearer one.

**[authored]** The test, at the threshold, extending §3's: stand in the approach cell and, blind, write the trade, then one line on the front — do its parts belong to one construction, and which part jars if any — then, for a frame onto a square or a yard, the name of what stands in it. A frame answered with a jarring part, or a landmark named wrong or not nameable, is a finding at the place's program (spec-0123 §3.4).

**[authored]** What the engine measures, and what it does not. It states, per bound piece, whether each door and gate stands framed — its two jambs at both courses and the cell over its head, a gate's two neighbours, each a cell a body cannot be in by the nav model's one predicate, recorded present or absent — and per face the openings it finds with each one's head course and width, the block kinds a face is built of with the longest unbroken run of one kind, and where the piece's water stands against the walk plane. These are measurements the review reads beside the threshold frame, and none is a refusal: a door standing free in a ruin, a gate where a hedge line ends, a hierarchy of heads, a third kind with a job and a flat ring are each an intent the author may mean and did not declare, and a check never infers an undeclared intent in order to refuse. The module, the construction and the leaf are never checked: they are read.

## 8. The plot: what a place holds

**[ruling]** A plot is a whole holding. One plot holds one or more structures that are closely related — by what they are for, or by design — together with the open spaces they share: a house with its front ground, its back ground and its outbuilding; a forge with its work yard and store; a farm with its house, barn, byre and yard; a shrine with its gate, approach, basin and halls; a quay with its apron, warehouse and shed; a camp with its rampart strip, headquarters, barracks and granary. The suburban lot is one example and names nothing in the rule. When the site is cut, each plot is planned with what it contains — which structures, which open spaces and whether each is walked or only seen, and what share each takes — and the plot's size follows from that plan. Inside the plot the author builds the planned contents freely, in any outline. A plot of one structure and no ground is legitimate.

**[ruling]** The scale is the player building community's, never the real world's and never the game's own villages. The game's village houses are too small to hold story content and are at most a lower bound. Real-world lot planning says which parts belong together and how they are arranged, and never sets a size: Minecraft differs too much from reality. The primary source for the size, the style and the composition of a plot and its structures is what recognised builders and build teams make and teach.

### 8.1 The community's rules of scale

**[cited]** The body is 0.6 wide and 1.8 tall, its eye at 1.62, its jump about 1.25 blocks. (Player.) A ceiling of two blocks stops a jump and reads as cramped; three to four feels spacious; many builders hold four to five as a small room's height; a dwelling's main room runs five to six, a hall ten to twelve, with the other rooms of the same building at four to five; hallways are two wide in a small house and three or four in a large one; a window one or two blocks over the floor is easy to see out of. (Adding beauty.) A two-block ceiling is the applicant's mistake named first; rooms are planned by purpose before any wall stands, a large open room is divided, and a yard is a square space behind the house where the plot allows. (WesterosCraft, Interiors.) The footprint is laid out in wool and judged from outside for scale and from inside for its rooms before anything is built; a plain square reads flat, and an L, a T or an offset is added only where the added section has a function. (WesterosCraft, Layout.) An applicant to a build team is set a farmhouse first, then a trade building named by region and profession — a forge, a homestead — and later a whole farmstead, and is judged on whether the interior suits the profession and the class. (WesterosCraft, Apply.) Depth before anything: overhanging eaves, a recessed door, steps at the base, panes for glass, and then the garden and the ground round the house — ponds, a patch of farm, flowers of mixed height. (Make Your Houses Better, after Avomance.) A village's buildings each connect to the street and not to each other; a town is denser than a village and groups like services; a settlement reads as genuine with a market or small trade shops. (Settlement guide.) The game's own villagers need a hut of 5 × 5 and a trading hall three high. (Creating a village.)

### 8.3 How a program sets a size

**[cited]** A building's area is programmed before it is drawn: the spaces it needs are listed with an area each, the areas are summed, and a multiplier for the ground between them grosses the sum to the whole; one multiplier for every project is the recorded misconception, because the share the ground between takes depends on how open or how enclosed the plan is. (WBDG.) **[authored]** In the game the same order holds, with the community's numbers for the areas: list the parts, size each from §8.2, add the walls and the ways, and the plot is the sum.

### 8.4 How real holdings are composed — arrangement only, never a size **[cited]**

A setback keeps a structure off the line and the street for privacy, access and uniform appearance (Setback). A lot holds its main building toward the street and its accessory building — garage, shed — in the rear yard only, off the side and rear lines, never between the house and the street, and smaller than the main building (Montgomery County; Toronto). A trade plot is narrow at the street and deep into the block: the shop faces the street with its goods out front, the household's rooms lie behind and above, and a small court sits midway for light (Shophouse). A farmyard is the ground beside the barn, fenced by the farm's own buildings, with the animals and a trough in it (Farmyard); farmsteads are courtyard, L, U, linear or dispersed plans, and the house is attached to the working range or detached and set away, often facing away from the yard into its own garden (Shropshire). A shrine precinct is a sequence along its approach — gate, path, basin, lanterns, the hall laypeople enter, and behind it the small closed inner hall in its own fence (Shinto shrine); a close is enclosed ground round the great building with the serving houses at its edge (Cathedral close); a bailey is a palisaded yard holding hall, kitchens, chapel, barracks, stores, stables and workshops under its mound (Motte-and-bailey); a camp is a rectangle with two crossing ways, the headquarters at the crossing, barracks in paired blocks, granaries central and a clear strip inside the rampart (Castra); a quay is a platform with berths and warehouses behind (Wharf). Every figure these records carry — feet, metres, percentages — stays with them; none crosses into the game.

### 8.5 The rule **[authored, from the rulings, the community's rules and the measured builds]**

A plot is planned in this order, and its box is the last thing written:

1. **Name the parts.** Each part is a **structure** — a roofed, walled volume a body may enter — or **ground** — open space under the sky. Each is named by its use in the author's words, and nothing in the engine's surface carries a building type: the kind is the mechanism, the use is the author's.
2. **Say which parts a body walks** and which are only seen. A work yard, an approach and a shop's front are walked; a garden strip behind a hedge, a closed inner hall and a store nobody enters are seen. The question CLAUDE.md asks of every space — what does the player do in here — is answered per part at the cut, and a part with no answer is cut.
3. **Give each part its play space in cells**, the cells a body could stand in, exactly as a box's extent is a play space: a structure's interior floor, a ground part's open cells. The bands are the community's middle half (§8.2): a dwelling 80 to 220 cells; a structure a trade is worked or served in — a shop and workroom, an inn, a bakery — 120 to 320; a barn, a stable or a store 90 to 270; a chapel or a hall 190 to 460; a shed, a well-house or a tower foot 8 to 30. A ground part is sized by what is done in it: a front strip two to four cells deep across the frontage; a back yard or work yard about the play space of the structure it serves; a court or a close about as large as the structures that face it together; a camp's clear strip two to three cells inside the wall. Where a part's height is a design fact, state it in courses from the walk plane to the ridge: the corpus puts a dwelling's ridge at 16 (7 to 32), an inn's at 13, a barn's at 10 or 11, a church's at 17, and a storey at four to five with a main room at five to six (§8.1).
4. **Sum, and add the walls and the ways.** Each structure takes a one-cell ring for its walls and the corpus's eave beyond it; the parts are joined by ground a body walks, at least two cells wide, from the plot's entry to every walked part; a structure stands clear of the plot's line by its ring at least, and further where a side yard or an eave is wanted. The plot's play space is the sum of the parts' cells, the rings and that ground, and no more: a plot whose structures and walked ground cover less than about half of it is oversized, and the remainder is spread between its structures or the plot is cut down. The community's density is a town's, denser than a village's with its buildings meeting the street (§8.1), so a street lot stands at 50 to 80 % built and walked, a trade frontage near 100 % less its court, a farmstead or a close lower because its yard is the point of it.
5. **Place by the arrangements of §8.4, not by a rule per theme.** The entry side is the front: a structure's door faces it, a trade's front stands at or near the line, a dwelling stands back by a strip of ground. An outbuilding stands to the rear or the side of its main structure, never between it and the entry, at least one cell from it so both read as buildings. A yard that serves a structure is enclosed by it and by the plot's line, so the structure is one side of the yard. A precinct is a sequence along one axis from the gate to the innermost part, the innermost smallest and last. A camp or a close is a wall round a clear strip round its structures.
6. **Then write the box**: a sky-open place whose extent is the derived play space and whose courses of air clear the tallest structure's ridge; its sides and seams as any place's. A structure that is the whole plot — no ground, the outline the rectangle — may be written as today's lidded box instead, and is then not a plot.

**[authored]** The size test, before the box is written: can a body standing at the plot's entry name every part and say what it would do in each; does any part fall under the community's lower quartile or exceed its upper quartile by half again; is the remainder over a third. A yes to the second or third is the oversized or the undersized plot the rulings refuse.

### 8.6 What the engine measures, and what it does not **[authored]**

The engine holds a plot to its own program and refuses nothing it did not declare (spec-0125). At validation it confirms the plot can hold its parts — the parts' cells fit the play space, the tallest declared height fits the courses of air, and the box is sky-open. At detail it confirms each declared part is built: the place's program names the part each of its spaces and out-of-walk regions belongs to, every declared part is named by at least one, a walked part has a cell the body reaches, and the part's built play space is within a quarter of its declared cells. It states, per plot, the built coverage, the unprogrammed remainder, each structure's setback on each side, the gaps between structures, each structure's storey clearances and ridge height, and the share of each part's columns that stand under a roof; the review reads these against §8.1, §8.2 and §8.5. Whether the parts belong together, whether the yard answers what the player does in it, whether a structure reads as its use from the entry and whether the holding reads as one holding are read from the threshold frame drawn per part, blind, as §7's test is read; none is measured.

How a plot is drawn so these confirmations hold without the author reading the engine: the place's generator draws every structure, yard and piece of life into the skill's starter (`scripts/starter.py`, `Model`) as cells, names each part's columns, and lets the starter claim the cells from the engine's own answers (`delvec grammar survey`) — the floor a body reaches into its part, the floor no body reaches (a roof, a wall head, a sill) out of walk, a walk between two parts only where a body crosses (carrying the `rise` between their floors where they differ), a part none of whose floor a body reaches declared seen and never entered, an open side's ring cell beside a wall or a hedge the plot's own air. Its block constructors (`stairs`, `slab`, `pillar`, `door`, `trapdoor`, `fence`, `fence_gate`, `wall`, `pane`, `lantern`, `leaves`, `bed`, `candle`, `wall_torch`, `campfire`, `ladder`, `chain`, `barrel`, `button`, `grass_block`, `tall_plant`) write every property, and `Model.door`, `Model.bed` and `Model.tall` stand both halves; `Model.roof` lays a gable (or, `hip=True`, a hipped) roof whose eave course sits level with the wall heads, so the loft under it closes. Under an opening two or more courses over the walk plane the starter stands a pillar, a landing and a ladder; a drawing that climbs there itself — a flight, a ramp — says so with `Model.climbs_to(edge)`, so no ladder stands in the middle of its steps **[ruling]**, and one that builds there without saying so is refused. The starter refuses, before anything is written, a block the server drops, a block the palette refuses, and a state the pinned game does not have or that leaves a property out — the engine's own registry answers, through the same survey. It needs a `delvec` binary on the machine that runs the generator; the handout lists the refused blocks and the blocks the holder census counts as containers (`delvec grammar blocks`).

## 10. A town

Reader: the creator laying out a settlement at step 2 and each place's author at step 9. A town is a site plan like any other map; these are the rules a whole town was judged by, each marked as above. The mechanisms that draw them are the skill starter's town helpers (`scripts/town.py`, re-exported by `starter.py`; `tools.md`); the design — palettes, which trade gets which front, the coast — stays the campaign's.

1. **The island is disguised, and its edge is dressed.** **[ruling]** A town on an island reads as a peninsula of a larger land: the playable side faces the sea, the far side returns the player. **[cited]** The region may reach past every box, and what a player sees from the walk region's edge is `16 × (view_distance + 1)` blocks of land and sea (`compiler.md`, *region*; `town.seen_region`). **[authored]** So the fill lays ground out to that reach on every side; past the walk region the land runs on and falls to the sea over a varying distance — headlands and coves, never a straight coast — and a summit castle stands on its mountain in steps of rock, never on a table cut flat to its box.
2. **A street is a place.** **[ruling]** A connector is a building with its own box: paved, furnished and lit, never a flat stand-in. **[cited]** A medieval street was cobbled and drained by a gutter, the *kennel* (Street gutter); a harbour town's main street falls steeply to the harbour, cobbled and closed to carts (Clovelly). **[authored]** The clear way is paved with its kennel down the middle and worn in patches; steps carry the street down in half-courses with a landing at each foot; the verge outside the clear width holds the street's life — lamps, benches, barrels, a cart, beds of flowers — clear of every opening and doorway (`town.stretch`, `flight`, `lane`, `ramp`, `rail`).
3. **Seams are seamless, and walls belong to buildings.** **[ruling]** A plot is only the author's attention partition: no boundary marker, no wall drawn because a box ends; a wall stands because a building or a yard has it. **[authored]** A way's paving runs to the box edge and the neighbour's ground meets it at the sill (§6); a lot's edge column never stands over air (`town.close_edge_gaps`), and an eave stops at the play space's edge (`town.clip_beyond`, `clip_unwalled_edge`).
4. **A front stands on the street, met at its doorway.** **[cited]** A town's buildings meet the street (Settlement guide, §2). **[authored]** A lot that fronts its street meets it only over its doorway: the plan cuts the seam to the doorway's span (`town.front_contact`), the lot's building front stands on its edge, and every other edge cell up to the street's air is the front's wall or a yard wall with its coping (`town.street_seam`, `street_front`). A trade's sign hangs on the street's own bracket before the doorway, lettered by the campaign (`town.bracket`, `sign_station`; spec-0128 §8).
5. **Buildings are set back, and windows look onto open space.** **[ruling]** Buildings stand back from the plot's and the map's edges, and a window looks onto a yard, a street or the sea, never into a neighbour's wall. **[cited]** A setback keeps light, air and access between buildings (Setback, §8.4).
6. **An interior is several rooms.** **[ruling]** A building is entered through a first room — a shop, a reception — and the work room behind it is joined by a door; L and U outlines, porches and open-walled work fronts are the author's. **[cited]** Rooms have purposes and are placed as a household used them (§3).
7. **The outside is lived in.** **[ruling, cited]** Bushes, flower pots, small ornaments and yards round the houses (§4).
8. **Each trade from its own references.** **[ruling]** A building is drawn from community reference builds of its theme, recorded per place as the node's `references[]` — never a set of similar houses told apart by a few function blocks (§3). **[authored, from the constitution's rule that a craft question is researched against established practice]** Where the community has no build of the trade, the trade's real building is taken from a record of it (a museum, a historic-environment record, an encyclopedia entry on the trade or its working object) for where the work stands and how the rooms divide, and the scale, palette and front from community builds of the nearest trade sharing its arrangement; each row says which it is, and one row records that no build of the trade was found and what was searched. A page that cannot be read is not read by other means; a row never read is no reference (`references/detail.md` step 0).
9. **One style, at the community's scale.** **[ruling]** The town shares one palette family and one scale, the player-building community's, not the game's villages (§7, §8.1).
10. **The interior camera stands in a room's corner.** **[authored]** A frame of an interior is taken from a corner of the room at standing eye height, looking across it, at a field of view of about 90°, so the room, its doors and its furniture are in one frame; a frame from the middle of a room shows a wall.
11. **A whole map is looked at as a set of overviews.** **[ruling]** Before a town is called done it is looked at from the air, from its summit, from the sea and at eye height along its main street; each view is where a defect of the whole shows that no single place's frame shows (a straight coast, a table under a castle, a strip where a street is a stand-in).
12. **Every place without a program is counted before a map is called done.** **[ruling]** A place still standing in its stand-in is a hole in the town. **[authored]** The count is the places `delvec allocation <campaign> --all` prints less the stems under `<campaign>/programs/`, each named; a map is done at zero.
