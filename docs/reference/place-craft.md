# A place reads as what it is

Reader: the agent writing one place's program at step 9, and the engine work that hands it a frame. This is the research record behind spec-0123: how big a building is cut, how it is divided into rooms, how it shows its trade from the threshold and from the street, what makes its outside look lived in, what a way owes its approach, and how the parts of one building are kept in one style so that a front, and a landmark, read as what they are (§7). Every rule is marked **[cited]** with its source, **[authored]** — reasoned here from a cited rule or from the engine, with no source of its own — or **[ruling]** — a finding of the side-by-side playtest of one hillside town built twice, taken as given. Sources are read for their rules; nothing of their text, images or builds is taken (ADR-0013: an unlicensed or non-commercial source is ideas only). Rooflines and facades are `roof-and-facade-craft.md`; light is `interior-lighting.md`; the width of a way is `clear-width.md`.

## 2. The cut and the size

**[ruling]** A site is cut with **one box per building or connector**; this cut's layout and seams were judged better than a cut of two terraces. A road, a flight of steps, a bridge, a yard and a square's centrepiece are each a place with a box of their own.

**[cited]** A village's buildings each connect to the street and not usually to each other; a town has larger open centres and denser structures; a settlement reads as genuine when it has a market place or, failing that, small trade shops. (Settlement guide.) A yard — a square space out back for vegetables, washing, animals or storage — is part of a house where the plot allows. (WesterosCraft.)

**[ruling]** A building's play space is **about twice** what the first cut gave it, so its author has room for rooms. The first cut's trade buildings stood on 64 to 121 cells of play space (8 × 8 to 11 × 11, read from its site plan); the rule puts a trade building at about 130 to 240 cells.

**[authored]** From the ruling and §3's room rule: a trade building's play space is at least **12 × 12**, and at least **14 × 14** where a workshop and a shop front both stand; a one-room house may stay smaller. These are the numbers a geometry brief states per trade and a site plan binds with `identities[]` (`box-extent`, `ge`), so the size is a declaration the plan is held to and never a class the engine infers.

## 3. Rooms, and reading as a trade

**[cited]** Divide the interior into distinct rooms with specific purposes before any wall goes up; place them as a household used them — work and storage downstairs, sleeping upstairs, the kitchen at the hearth; a single undivided room rarely reads as a lived-in space, and a two-block ceiling reads as cramped. (WesterosCraft.) Ceilings of three to four blocks feel spacious; hallways are two wide in a small house and three or four in a large one; a kitchen is a raised table, a sink, a range and a worktop, a bedroom a bed, a chest dresser and something on the wall. (Adding beauty.)

**[cited]** Most householders worked from home: a carpenter's downstairs workshop, a blacksmith's tools at the hearth, a weaver's loom. Every block serves a purpose; a small detail that shows occupancy — one mug on a barrel — beats a pile of crates that only takes up space. (WesterosCraft.)

**[cited]** In environmental storytelling the elements combine into a larger picture while each has significance on its own; the player assembles *what happened here* from the objects and their arrangement, and a scene that says nothing about the premise wastes its place. (Worch and Smith.)

**[cited]** For a public that could not read, a picture hung over the street named the trade — the bush of a tavern, the pole of a barber, the three balls of a pawnbroker; hanging signs became so common that cities limited their size and projection. (Signage.)

**[ruling]** A plain house with a loom and wool dumped in it does **not** read as a weaver's. An open-walled smithy, and a cartographer's shop with a reception room in front of an inner map room, do read as their trades. The best single room seen was a spawn-side room with a table and carpet.

**[authored]** The rule, from the four citations and the ruling. A building reads as its trade when **four things hold at once**:

1. **The working object stands in its working position**, not stored: the loom with a web on it and the bench before it, the anvil beside the forge with the quench at hand, the drawing table under the window with the finished map on the wall. A tool on a shelf is storage; a tool in use is a trade.
2. **The rooms follow the work**: the room a customer enters is not the room the work is done in, and the door between them says so — a counter room and a workroom, a shop and a store, a kitchen and a hall. A trade building has at least two rooms.
3. **The front says the trade before the door is opened**: a sign of the trade's own object hung over the street, the goods shown at a window or a counter, or the workroom itself open to the street where the trade was worked in the open (a smithy, a stall).
4. **Every room has a use a player could name**, and nothing is filler: a crate stack, a lone furnace, a bare bed in a shop are the cells the ruling judged bad.

**[authored]** The test, at the threshold: stand in the door and name the trade with no sign read. A frame from the threshold is the engine's picture of this test (spec-0123 §3.4), and the review names the trade from the frame before it reads the label.

## 4. Exterior life

**[cited]** A garden of shrubs, flowers, a few trees and potted plants; a hedge of one or two leaf blocks on a log; stairs and slabs as edging; worn floors in cracked and mossy variants where feet pass; a wall that looks empty gets something hung on it. (Adding beauty.) An animal pen, a vegetable patch or a wood-chopping place gives a plain house character. (WesterosCraft.)

**[ruling]** The first cut had too few of these: bushes, potted flowers and non-functional props that make the outside feel lived in.

**[authored]** Where the outside lives in this engine: a street's verge (the columns of a way place's extent outside its `clear`, `clear-width.md` §3), a yard or garden strip cut as its own open-sided place (spec-0123 §5), and the commons dressed by the stage-7 edit script (`scatter`, `plant`, `fragment`). Nothing stands in a way's clear width; a prop against a house wall stands in the street's verge, so the street's author dresses the house's foot, and the handout tells it what house it is.

## 5. The approach to a wide way

**[cited]** A stairway landing is as wide as the stair it serves and as deep as the stair's width (to 48 inches); a door onto it never takes more than half of it. (IBC §1011.6, through `clear-width.md`.) The engine confirms a way's declared width along its whole corridor and **one approach cell** at each end (spec-0120 §3).

**[ruling]** A three-wide grand stair with a one-wide crooked approach in one room is a defect; in the case seen a newel and a fireplace left one cell. A wide way's approach is as wide as the way, at least three; features such as a hearth and a stair stand three to five cells apart.

**[authored]** The engine judges the width and the first approach cell; the depth of the approach beyond that cell is the author's, from the ruling: a landing as deep as the way is wide, and nothing fixed — a hearth, a newel, a pillar — inside that landing. A newel stands beside the stair's width (`clear-width.md` §3), and a hearth's cheeks stand outside the landing.

## 6. The ground at a seam

**[cited]** A change in level above half an inch on an accessible route is a ramp, not a step (ADA 303); a change of less than twelve inches in a path of travel is a slope, and a single riser is admitted only where a code names it. (IBC §1003.5.)

**[ruling]** At a seam between places the ground must not leave a trench that a body walking the main road falls into and must detour to get out of.

**[authored]** The ring row across a seam at grade is walked at the sill: both pieces draw their ground to the sill at the crossing, and the side the plan hands the row to paves it. A cell of a way's corridor lower than the cells before and after it along the travel is a trench; `DW0921` asks a different question — whether a body is soft-locked — and a trench a body can jump out of is not a pocket, so its zero is not evidence against the ruling. What a check would measure is spec-0123 §11.

## 7. One building, one style

**[ruling]** The parts of one building, and of one frontage, are visually coherent with each other: in proportion, in height rhythm, and in the consistency of material and element. The choice of door, opening or counter follows from how that building is constructed, and a different way of building makes a different door the right one. A landmark must read as what it is. The case judged: an open smithy front whose counter stood one course high with a two-course door beside it, both one cell in from a five-course open face with air over both, so the door read as abrupt; and a square's fountain, a flat ring of stone round one cell of water at the rim's own height, which read as a well. The rule is systemic and is **not** written per building type — no "a smithy takes a fence gate".

**[cited]** Order gives due measure to the members of a work considered separately; eurythmy is beauty and fitness in the adjustments of the members, when they all correspond symmetrically; symmetry is a proper agreement between the members of the work itself, and the relation between the different parts and the whole, in accordance with a certain part selected as standard. (Vitruvius.) Beauty results from the form and correspondence of the whole with respect to the several parts, of the parts with regard to each other, and of these again to the whole; walls are built so that the solid stands upon the solid and the void over the void, the openings of doors and windows exactly over one another; a principal door is about twice as high as it is wide; the windows of one storey are all equal, though the rooms behind them differ; the storey above has windows a sixth less in the height of the void than those beneath; a jamb is no less than a sixth and no more than a fifth of the opening. (Palladio.) Relationships between a building's parts are governed by multiples of a standard unit, the module. (Proportion.) A facade is counted in bays, the space between posts or columns, and where there are only regularly spaced windows each window is one bay. (Bay.)

**[cited]** Openings define a building's character by their shape and their rhythm — a run grouped 4, 3, 4, 1, 4 over a run of 1, 1, 1 — and by the relationship between the width of the openings and the wall space between them; inserting a window into the gap, or painting the window heads to match the wall, changes the whole; a modest front may have a three-part shape whose centre bay is the controlling aspect; a hierarchy makes the front's windows more important than the side's; a rhythm is so much of a thing's character that breaking a few of its repeats alters it. (Brief 17.) A storefront is bulkheads, display windows, transoms, a signboard under a cornice, and the piers or thin iron posts that frame them; its entrance is differentiated from the others and often recessed; "most storefront frames are generally composed of horizontal and vertical elements"; a new front respects the scale and proportion of the building, keeps the horizontal separation between the storefront and the storeys above, places its doors where they relate to the windows above, avoids too many different colours on one facade and carries one colour scheme over every painted element to unify upper and lower; the question asked of any front is whether it and the floors above were created as an overall design or are very different and unrelated; a front dressed in a borrowed vocabulary — "lumberyard colonial" on a commercial building — is wrong whatever its craft. (Brief 11.)

**[cited]** The shopfront descends from the market stall set into the ground floor: a medieval shopfront was two or more unglazed openings with arched heads and shutters, with a low shelf pegged to the structure; the unglazed front stayed an integral part of the building's structural frame and its cill grew into a fully developed counter; a shutter hinged at the bottom folded down to form the counter; pilasters were introduced to carry the upper storeys; when plate glass arrived the upper storeys looked as if they sat unsupported on a void, and pilasters, consoles and cornices were added to terminate the front and give it visual support — a heavy structure on a flimsy sheet below is invariably unsuccessful. The stallriser was the display table and forms a solid base; its material matches the shopfront and the building above; the cill's size depends on the scale and proportion of the other details; the door's design reflects the other elements; the console's choice reflects the details used in the other elements; the fascia is in proportion to the shopfront and to the height of the building and sits below the first-floor cills. (Canterbury; buildinghistory.) A lintel is the beam that spans an opening, an arch is a curved lintel, and a sill lies below. (Lintel.) A Dutch door is divided so that the bottom leaf may remain shut while the top leaf opens — a door that is also a window, which kept animals out and let light and air in. (Dutch door.)

**[cited]** In the game: one colour of timber and daub per build, two complementary woods at most, no long unbroken run of one block, and depth only with a structural reason — a sill, a reveal, a buttress, the eave — because depth that only breaks a flat wall looks arbitrary (WesterosCraft; held in `roof-and-facade-craft.md` §6); the palette is carried into the roof, so a house of oak and spruce planks gets no acacia ceiling; depth is crucial; a window one or two blocks above the ground is easy to see out of; too many doors make a house look designed rather than lived in. (Adding beauty.) The game's own well, the plains meeting point, is a cobblestone ring round four cells of water two deep, the ring standing two courses above the ground, four fence posts two tall on it and a cobblestone slab for a roof; the game's own fountain is a cobblestone stack with a water source on its top at the third course, pouring down. (Village blueprints.) The community's fountain is a frame of at least 5 × 5 round a column three high with water poured on the column's top so that it runs down every side, the frame at least four wider than the column, placed at the centre of a garden, a yard or a park. (Building water features.)

**[cited]** A fountain is a decorative reservoir used for discharging water, or a structure that jets water into the air; until the modern pump every fountain ran by gravity, so its source stood higher than it, and a village fountain kept a vessel of water above its spouts so the water fell from the spouts into the basin below. (Fountain.) A well's lining is extended upward above the ground to form a wall around the hole, a roof is hung over it, and the water is raised in a bucket. (Water well.) A landmark is an external point of reference at least one aspect of which is unique or memorable in its context; the most successful node is unique in some way. (Image of the City; Imageability.) What reads is judged from the player's own position — an oblique angle makes a door nearly invisible; a screenshot taken to black and white and blurred shows where the eye lands; pretend to be a new player. (Readability.)

**[authored]** The rule, from the citations and the ruling, in four parts. A building is in one style when all four hold:

1. **One module.** Every part of a building is measured in the unit the body gives it: a **course** (a counter, a sill, a step, a stallriser), a **body** of two courses (a door, a window's clear height, the gap under a beam a body walks through), a **storey** (the clearance). Parts that share a wall share the unit and meet at its lines: the counter's top is the door's hand-height, the door's head is the window's head, the storey line is the eave or the beam. A part that stands in no line — a one-course counter and a two-course door with nothing over either under a five-course void — is the miss the ruling names.
2. **Openings follow construction.** An opening is a hole in a wall, so it has jambs and a head; what fills it follows how the wall is built, and the leaf is chosen last. A walled front takes a door in the wall and a window beside it. A front that is a frame of posts and a beam takes a counter under the beam and no door — the body walks round the counter's end, as at a stall. A timber-framed front takes a wide shuttered opening whose sill has grown into the counter, and a half-door beside it under the same head. A masonry front takes an arch with the counter under it. A front with no wall at all — an open side — must build the frame that the wall would have been, one cell inside the open ring, and stand its counter and its door in that frame: a door standing in air is a door with no wall, and the engine refuses it (spec-0123 §3.5). No door is named per trade; the construction names it.
3. **Lines run through.** The heads of a front's openings stand at one course, or in a hierarchy the author means (the principal door taller than the rest, Palladio's rule), and a horizontal — the beam, the fascia, the eave — terminates the front over every opening; posts stand under posts, solid upon solid and void over void. The trim that frames one opening frames every opening on that front; one structural material and one infill per building, and a facade of more than three block kinds is read for whether each kind has a job. A front and the storeys above it are one design, never two.
4. **A landmark reads by its silhouette from the approach.** A body reads shape before detail, at eye height, from the cell where it enters the place; what the thing does must be visible in the silhouette. Water that stands above the ground and falls says fountain; water below a rim under a roof on posts says well; a figure on a plinth above eye height says statue. The parts of a landmark obey parts 1–3; a flat ring round a cell of water at the rim's own height is neither shape and reads as the nearer one.

**[authored]** The test, at the threshold, extending §3's: stand in the approach cell and, blind, write the trade, then one line on the front — do its parts belong to one construction, and which part jars if any — then, for a frame onto a square or a yard, the name of what stands in it. A frame answered with a jarring part, or a landmark named wrong or not nameable, is a finding at the place's program (spec-0123 §3.4).

**[authored]** What the engine measures, and what it does not. It measures that a door has a wall: every door's two jambs at both courses and the cell over its head, and every fence gate's two neighbours, are cells a body cannot be in (the nav model's one predicate), because a door block is itself the declaration of an opening in a wall; a miss is refused. It states, per bound piece and per face, the openings it finds with each one's head course and width, the block kinds a face is built of with the longest unbroken run of one kind, and where the piece's water stands against the walk plane — measurements the review reads beside the threshold frame, refusing nothing, because whether a shared head line or a deliberate hierarchy was meant, whether three kinds each have a job, and whether a thing reads as a fountain are the author's intent and the reviewer's eye. The module, the construction and the leaf are never checked: they are read.
